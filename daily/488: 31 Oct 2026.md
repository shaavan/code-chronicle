## Reflection

The previous pass left me with an implementation plan and several questions
about where OMv2 meets existing behavior. Today I went back through the proposal
more slowly. I could recognize route blinding and onion construction in the
design, but following the packet still meant stopping at fields whose names
were easier to recognize than to explain. Updating the references was a small
start. From there, I worked through one question at a time.

The fixed slots gave me somewhere concrete to begin. Every hop receives the
same eight-slot header, removes its first slot, and adds filler at the end.
Seeing seven slots after that operation does not reveal seven remaining hops;
the useful encrypted slots and filler cannot be distinguished. I had already
covered the layout in the earlier review. This time I needed to understand
what a node could learn while processing it, including why the final node
needs the zero next HMAC to recognize delivery.

Route blinding took most of the attention. I used Alice sending through Bob,
Charlie, and Dave to Eve, with Eve supplying a blinded path beginning at
Charlie. Alice knows Charlie as the introduction node. Beyond that, blinded
identities let her construct the onion, while encrypted instructions let
Charlie find Dave and Dave find Eve. Keeping those two jobs in view helped me
stop treating the blinded path as one opaque object.

The keys were where I kept having to slow down. A node's identity, its blinded
identity, the packet's temporary onion key, and the separate path key each
needed a place in the example. Going back to Diffie–Hellman helped: multiplying
either private key by the other side's public key reaches the same curve point.
That gave the derivations something I could follow. The path setup produces
the blinding material and `rho_path` for forwarding instructions. The onion
setup uses the blinded identity to produce `rho`, `mu`, and `delta` for the
header, HMAC, and payload. A recipient can prepare its hidden route before
the sender constructs a fresh message through it.

I then followed a route Alice prepares herself, deriving keys forwards and
building the packet backwards. At Bob, I could ask which secret he derives,
which HMAC he checks, which layer he removes, and what he forwards to Charlie.
The HMAC stored inside his slot belongs to Charlie; the outer one belongs to
the packet Bob received. The instruction's authentication tag has its own
job too. Even the two public keys outside the HMAC's direct input affect the
authentication key derived for checking it. Tracing one hop made those layout
details easier to hold together.

By the end, I was asking smaller questions again. The final slot reserves
32 bytes for `path_id` and leaves another 34 zeroed, so I drafted a question
about allowing recipient-defined context across that space. An unfamiliar
channel-only feature turned out to come from existing BOLT definitions; a
reference would save the next reader that detour. I have not implemented or
validated the protocol yet. I can now read more of the packet without having
to skip over how its keys and fields get used.
